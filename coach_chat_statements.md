# Coach statements made in conversation (context beyond the questionnaires)

These are things Lalo Vera said directly while building the model (2026-10-07 to 2026-10-09), paraphrased closely. They sit alongside `coach_answers.md` (his earliest chat statements) and the three questionnaire rounds. Where a later questionnaire answer conflicts with one of these, the later answer wins.

## What the project is for
- He wants his programming system captured so a tool can take basic information and "kick out" a 6-, 8- or 12-week plan (later: 4–6-week maintenance, 8, 12, week-by-week, yearly).
- "The hardest thing to understand is the categorization of everything": bigger muscles, smaller muscles, biomechanics; athletic patterns, resistance patterns, core.
- His lineage: Dr. Pat Davidson, Dr. Mike Israetel, and training science from first principles. He is deliberately not building on packaged models (5/3/1, Texas Method, Prilepin's chart). He is "building the segmentation and the buckets from the ground up."
- The MASTER EXERCISE LIBRARY in the "BUILD YOUR OWN PROGRAM" sheet is the main thing to understand.
- It should serve "different gyms, different people, different coaches, and all of our clients": a database "to help a lot of people who haven't really created something like this."

## How he thinks about athletes
- "Different athletes need different things." Basketball players need more plyos; boxers and karate athletes don't need that many; MMA fighters need more than boxers but fewer than basketball players. "Play with the volume."
- Some athletes need more resistance training; others need more power and explosiveness through athletic patterns.
- "For the athletes, all of the different popular sports in the United States" must be there, "include swimming as well."
- General population: include pickleball ("very popular right now") and HYROX. "They want to stay athletic and active, so we need to understand that that's an option for them."

## Volume
- People do about 15–20 sets per muscle per week. More for a muscle they want to grow, fewer with injury or pain.
- Volume ramps week by week in small increments (he first said 3%, 5% or 7%).
- Smaller muscles take more reps because you can't go heavy; bigger muscles take a mix. Everyone works between 5 and 30 reps.
- Some people prefer more sets with less weight; some prefer powerlifting style (heavier, fewer reps). Adjust to the goal.
- "I usually go week to week: how many sets did you do this week? If you want to do more, do more sets the next week of the same thing." Example: 20 sets of chest this week; next week keep the load and do 22 sets with different exercises: more volume, more variety.
- "The volume between unilateral and bilateral is very important."

## Equipment and training variables
- Machines have many reference points and constraints (you sit, feet on the ground, move hands or feet). You can push a lot and it's safer. More machines for people who want more volume, people with injuries, and older people.
- Free weights are different: barbells are the most skilled; dumbbells, kettlebells and medicine balls sit in between. "If you read the workouts you can understand them by their equipment usage."
- Session intent order: power first (athletics), then power-strength, then strength, then resistance patterns.
- People will ask based on the equipment they have: "we want to be ready for that."
- Speed: to go faster you don't need a lot of weight. Medball upper-body passes should be light (8–10 lb). "The heavier I go, the slower I'm going to be. The lighter I go, ideally, the faster I can be." Quality of movement matters as much as quantity.
- Speed, range of motion (full, mid), load and volume are all variables.
- "Don't try to 'confuse' the muscle." Keep the same exercises over the block; in the next block change some small variables.
- Ordering: rowing (horizontal pull) before an overhead (vertical) pull; horizontal push before vertical push.

## Product and simplicity
- No landing page or app is being built yet. First organize the data and context.
- It must be very simple: put in the basics, get the whole program, see it ahead of time, adjust it yourself. No check-ins, no tracking, no data kept (confirmed in round 3).
- English first with a full Spanish toggle.
- Users: clients, coaches, trainers, strength and conditioning coaches, anyone.

## Open source and ownership
- The repo is private for now. It will become a fully explained public repo: "the most comprehensive training model that there is out there," free "for anybody to use in their own app and their own training."
- It may include copies of his original Google Sheets "so people can download it and play with it."
- People must be able to add and create exercises, with the repo explaining how. AI agents should be able to go in and grab from it.
- "I don't think anything like this exists." He believes only a handful of people have built something this robust and open-sourced it.
- The exercise videos linked in his sheets are not his. "The Blaze Camp name is the culture."

- He wants "the best mathematical, logical algorithms possible to make sure this is top of the line. Spare no expense."
