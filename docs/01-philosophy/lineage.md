# Lineage: where the ideas come from, and what he does not build on

This page explains the sources behind the Blaze Camp model. Coach Lalo Vera names three: Dr. Pat Davidson, Dr. Mike Israetel, and training science from first principles. He also says what he does not build on: packaged programs such as 5/3/1, the Texas Method and Prilepin's chart. The page keeps two things apart. The first is what the coach himself said about his sources. The second is the team's lineage map, which traced each idea in his sheets to a likely origin. The map is team analysis, not his words, and it is marked that way throughout.

Related pages: [principles](principles.md), [how he thinks](how-he-thinks.md), [what he rejected](what-he-rejected.md). The full lineage map with every reference is [synthesis/07_science_lineage.md](../../synthesis/07_science_lineage.md).

## The rule

**The model is the coach's own. It is built from the ground up on two named teachers and on basic training science. It is not a copy of anyone's packaged program, and research is used to check it, not to replace it.**

## 2. Dr. Pat Davidson: how the library is organized

**What comes from him.** The way exercises are sorted, and the idea that position decides difficulty.

**The coach's own statements.**
- The zones are positions with a rotation bias, taken from the book *A Coach's Guide to Optimizing Movement* (CAT-04, CAT-10). Arms down or overhead, or a deep squat, are vertical positions with an external-rotation bias. Hands in front, or the knee at hip height, is horizontal: "that is a zone 2 or an internal rotation bias, which is more compressive." (CAT-10).
- The zones also explain difficulty. Low and close to the body is easiest. Horizontal is "very high-pressure, like horizontal, but it's safer for you when you push and pull." Overhead "would be more advanced, with the positioning, the amount of stability that you need, and the skill that you need to do things right." (CAT-04).
- The zones are not for jumps: "It doesn't apply to the plyos. The plyos are different." (CAT-10).

**What his sheets show (OBSERVED).** Row 4 of his MASTER EXERCISE LIBRARY is labelled 'PATTERN'. It uses the three family names 'ATHLETIC', 'CONTROL' and 'RESISTANCE', and two more labels, 'CARDIO' and 'FUNCTIONAL'.

**What the team map adds (TEAM MAP, not his words).**

| Idea in the model | Davidson source, as the team traced it | How the coach uses it | Team verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Three families: control, athletic, resistance | Davidson's course *Rethinking the Big Patterns* sorts 13 movement patterns under the same three names | His library columns match 11 of the 13 patterns. Locomotion became his cardio column plus sprinting. Breathing is the one pattern he left out. | faithful; the strongest link in the system |
| Every exercise described by pattern, plane and stance | Davidson classifies exercises by pattern, plane, stance, load and speed | Principle 1: every exercise is tagged on every lens at once (CAT-01) | faithful |

It fits Davidson's wider model of expansion and compression. | A tag on each exercise. Both biases in every week. Used to pick a swap when something hurts (R2-LIB-02, R2-LIB-03). | adapted; recorded as the coach's account of the book |
| Level from load position | no published three-level scheme was found | Front-loaded is foundational, on the body is intermediate, behind the body is advanced; a seat, machine or cable makes it easier (CAT-04) | his own or adapted |

## 3. Dr. Mike Israetel: how much work

**What comes from him.** The way the work is dosed: sets per muscle per week, a wide rep range, effort measured by reps left in the tank, and small increases over time.

**The coach's own statements.** He named Israetel as an influence (COACH-CHAT). The rules themselves he gave in his own words:

| His rule | His words | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Count sets per muscle per week | "15 to 20 sets of any muscle group per week would be a good foundation for maintenance to a little bit of growth" | UNI-01 |
| More for a muscle to grow, fewer with pain | stated in conversation | COACH-CHAT |
| Everyone works between 5 and 30 reps | stated in conversation | COACH-CHAT |
| Effort by reps in reserve | "I like RIR in my model" | VOL-13 |
| Keep the same exercises for a block | "Don't try to 'confuse' the muscle." | COACH-CHAT, PER-02 |
| A deload is about half the sets | approved as proposed | PER-05 |

**What the team map adds (TEAM MAP, not his words).** The team matched these rules to Dr. Israetel's Renaissance Periodization (RP) material:

| RP idea, as the team summarized it | The coach's version | Same or different |
|---|---|---|
| Volume landmarks: a minimum that grows muscle, a range of best gains, a maximum you can recover from | starts 10 to 12, 15 to 20 or 20+ by level; a floor of 10; ceilings 14 / 22 / 25 (VOL-01, R2-VOL-06) | same idea, his numbers |
| Any rep count from about 5 to 30 can grow muscle; small muscles suit higher reps | small muscles 12 to 30, big muscles 5 to 15 (VOL-11) | same |
| Train 1 to 3 reps short of failure | 1 to 3 reps in reserve on every working set (VOL-13) | same |
| Pick exercises that give a lot of stimulus for little fatigue | His squat sheet notes that a leg press lets you focus on 'local fatigue of the quads/legs and not have so much systemic fatigue failure' (OBSERVED) | same |
| Add about one set per muscle per week, then deload every 4 to 6 weeks | **his own shape**: a stairway of about 3% then 5% steps over 8 or 12 weeks, with no deload inside the block (R2-PER-03, R2-VOL-07, R3-VOL-04) | different |
| Effort ramps from 3 reps in reserve down to 0 before the deload | 3, then 2, then 1 to 2, then 1 plus extra-hard sets; never below 1 on heavy compounds (R3-VOL-04, GEN-08) | similar, with his limit |
| (not from RP) | new sets go to the first exercise of the day first (VOL-07) | his own |
| (not from RP) | helper muscles count a quarter set (VOL-09) | his own |

In short, he takes Israetel's concepts and not Israetel's template (**TEAM MAP**).

## 4. First-principles science

**What it means here.** A first principle is a basic, well-tested fact about how the body adapts. He reasons from these facts instead of copying a finished program. The table links each principle to the rule it supports. The left column is the team's naming (**TEAM MAP**). The right columns are his rules, in his words.

| Principle (plain words) | His rule that rests on it | His words | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| The body adapts to exactly what you ask of it (specificity) | Different athletes need different things | "If the athlete has more plyo work in their sport, then we give them more plyo work." | ATH-01 |
| Heavy loads move slowly, light loads move fast (force and speed trade off) | Train speed with light loads | "The heavier I go, the slower I'm going to be. The lighter I go, ideally, the faster I can be." | COACH-CHAT |
| A quick stretch before a push adds force, and short contacts differ from long ones | Time on the ground decides plyometric versus ballistic | "The longer they're on the ground, the less it is going to be plyo, so it's more of a ballistic movement." | CAT-10 |
| Landing skill and a strength base come before intense jumping (plyometric guidelines) | Receive force before you create it | "Your body and your connective tissue need to learn how to receive forces before we teach it how to create." | CAT-10 |
| Muscle grows across a wide range of reps when the sets are hard | Everyone works between 5 and 30 reps; small muscles take more reps | stated in conversation; ranges confirmed | COACH-CHAT, VOL-11 |
| More weekly sets grow more muscle, up to a point | 10 to 25 sets per group per week | "I think doing 40 sets of chest per week might be way too much, but I think 5 might be too little." | UNI-01 |
| Sets must be hard to count, but failure adds fatigue | 1 to 3 reps in reserve | "every time they do a set, they do it correctly and they do it with effort." | VOL-14 |
| Load must rise gradually | The stairway | "It's hard to sustain a program like that for an 8-week program." (about 10% weekly jumps) | UNI-05 |
| More outside support means less stabilizing work | Machines for volume, injury and age | "the machine or the cable is doing the work for you." | CAT-04 |

Two of his rules are not in this table on purpose. Pairing opposite ends of the body and loading the spine heavy once a week per pattern are his own design. The team map found no outside source for them, so they are listed in section 7.

He asked the team to do the research rather than take his numbers on trust:

- "We need to figure out what's out there, what has been studied on this specific subject of volume, and see what kind of volume we can adjust." (UNI-01)
- "I believe so, but you can do some research and figure it out." (R2-VOL-06, about most people training 20 to 30 sets per muscle per week)
- "Let's make sure that we do it correctly and we give them good sound science here." (VOL-14)

### What the research check found (SCIENCE, team work)

| His number | What the research says | Team verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Beginners start at 10 to 12 sets | 10 or more weekly sets beat fewer for growth | matches |
| Intermediates at 15 to 20 | inside the range most studies find productive | matches as a working baseline |
| Growth at 20 to 25, advanced 20+ | still productive for trained people, with smaller returns | matches for trained people, for limited periods |
| A floor of 10 for every trained group | supported for growth blocks; maintenance needs far less | matches for training blocks, which is why maintenance is exempt (**PROPOSED** reading) |
| "most people train around that: 20 to 30 sets per muscle per week" (R2-VOL-06) | fits what trained people work up to when indirect work is counted; high as a description of most gym-goers | partly supported; no number in the model changed |
| "5 might be too little", "40 ... way too much" (UNI-01) | 5 is near the minimum useful dose; 40 gives little extra | matches |
| 10% a week is too much | no direct trial; small steps are favoured | supported indirectly |
| A deload at about half the sets | in the middle of what coaches and studies report | matches |
| Maintenance at 50 to 70% of the volume (PER-01) | well above the smallest dose studies found enough to hold muscle and strength; older adults need more of it | matches; the team keeps older adults at 70% (**PROPOSED**) |
| Test a 5-rep or 3-rep max, not a 1-rep max, from zero | a 5-rep max predicts the 1-rep max well | matches |
| Supersets to save time | paired sets save time with similar results | matches |
| A fixed plan with reps in reserve and no check-ins | adjusting load by reps in reserve does at least as well as fixed loads | matches |

The studies behind each row are listed in [synthesis/12_volume_algorithm_v1.md](../../synthesis/12_volume_algorithm_v1.md) (section 2) and in [BLAZE_CAMP_BRAIN.md](../../BLAZE_CAMP_BRAIN.md) (sections 5.8 and 15). Some sources in the lineage map could only be read through search summaries; the map says which ones.

## 6. What he deliberately does not build on

**The rule.** The model does not use a packaged program as its base. A packaged program is a finished, named system with fixed weeks, percentages or tables.

| Packaged model | What it is | His position | What the team found in his sheets (TEAM MAP) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5/3/1 (Wendler) | a four-week wave of fixed percentages | not used (COACH-CHAT) | no trace |
| Texas Method | a weekly volume day, light day and intensity day | not used (COACH-CHAT) | only a general heavy-day, light-day pattern, nothing specific |
| Prilepin's chart | a table of sets and reps for each percentage of a max | not used (COACH-CHAT) | his Olympic-lift numbers do not follow it |
| Conjugate (Westside Barbell) | max-effort and speed days with bands and chains | "I don't need it to be conjugate or anything like that" (PER-01) | one unfinished tab is labelled 'conjugate style training' and borrows the day structure; bands and chains are out of the model (EQ-12) |
| Israetel's own RP template | add one set a week, deload every 4 to 6 weeks | concepts used, template not (section 3) | his blocks have no deload inside (R2-VOL-07) |
| Any band or variable-resistance system | bands, chains, weight releasers | "I don't do bands." (EQ-01); variable resistance is parked "for now" (EQ-12) | archived, never used by the generator |

**Why.** He did not give a long reason. What he said is that he is building the categories himself (section 1), that the plans are not "anything set in stone" (PER-01), and that "this is all customizable." (R2-VOL-06). The team's reading (**PROPOSED**): a packaged program fixes the things he wants to stay open, such as who the person is, what their sport does and how the year is laid out.

**Two exceptions, stated plainly (TEAM MAP).** The half-marathon plan is Chris Hinshaw's, used as written (he confirmed this, PRG-12). The unfinished strength tabs reuse the Westside day structure. The team treats both as optional modules, not as his core logic.

## 8. Where his practice and the published evidence differ

A coach answer that someone disagrees with is not an error. It is recorded and left alone unless he changes it. These are the open differences the team noted (**TEAM MAP**):

| Topic | His practice | What the evidence or the source suggests | What the model does |
|---|---|---|---|
| Olympic lifts at 4 sets of 8 at 75% | written in his baseball program | high for a full lift with a catch | Settled by him: full squat cleans and snatches use 3 to 5 reps; the 8s stay for dumbbell, kettlebell and pull versions (R2-ATH-05) |
| French contrast with no rest inside | approved as proposed (ATH-12) | sources use short rests | kept as he approved it |
| One Blue, one Green, one Orange cardio session | his cheat sheet | endurance athletes train mostly easy | kept for strength and team clients; endurance athletes follow their own sport plan |
